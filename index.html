Cybersecurity Instructor

Served as a lab instructor for an undergraduate cybersecurity course, leading hands-on sessions for 30+ students per semester. My focus was not just on teaching tools, but on helping students think like security analysts and connecting technical findings to real-world threats and frameworks.

What I Taught

Modules covered network traffic analysis and packet inspection, vulnerability scanning and risk identification, threat detection and basic incident response concepts, and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework for structuring security practices. I helped translate these concepts into hands-on labs so students could apply theory in realistic scenarios.

Nmap Lab

Students performed network scanning and reconnaissance on controlled lab environments. They discovered live hosts on a subnet, identified open ports and running services, and interpreted scan results to understand potential attack surfaces. We discussed how attackers use this information during the reconnaissance phase and how defenders can detect or limit exposure.

Wireshark Lab

Students analyzed packet captures to understand network behavior and detect anomalies. A key focus was identifying suspicious patterns such as repeated DNS requests to unfamiliar domains indicating potential malware beaconing or C2 activity, unencrypted HTTP traffic containing sensitive data, and unusual traffic spikes to external IPs suggesting data exfiltration. I also trained students on detecting DNS exfiltration by filtering for high volumes of DNS queries, unusually long domain names with encoded data, frequent TXT record lookups, and periodic beaconing behavior. The goal was moving students from just reading packets to actually identifying abnormal behavior.

Nessus Lab

Students conducted vulnerability scans on lab systems and then prioritized findings based on CVSS severity scores, exploitability, and whether the affected system was internet facing or internal. Critical vulnerabilities on exposed services were addressed first, especially those with known exploits. This taught risk-based thinking rather than just scanning for the sake of it.

Python Incident Response Scripts

I developed Python scripts used across 4 class projects to simulate real SOC-style incident response workflows. Scripts took system or authentication logs as input and parsed them to flag indicators of compromise such as multiple failed login attempts from the same IP, logins at unusual times, and repeated access attempts across different accounts. The output was a structured summary that students used for further analysis, aligned with MITRE ATT&CK techniques. This introduced students to security automation and log-based threat detection.

Biggest Teaching Challenge

The hardest concept for students was distinguishing normal data from suspicious data. Many could run the tools but struggled to interpret results and determine which traffic or vulnerabilities actually mattered from a security perspective. I addressed this by focusing on patterns, context, and indicators of compromise rather than raw output, which directly contributed to a 20% improvement in lab completion rates.

MITRE ATT&CK Alignment

All lab exercises were framed around MITRE ATT&CK, a framework that maps attacker behavior and techniques across each stage of an attack, helping defenders detect, analyze, and respond to threats by understanding those patterns. Teaching these concepts at a fundamental level deepened my own threat-analysis skills and reinforced my ability to think both offensively and defensively.
